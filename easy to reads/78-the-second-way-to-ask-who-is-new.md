# 78 — The second way to ask who is new

Sentiel's remaining version-1 bar, S3, asks a blunt question. Given 48 tickets a
day — one shift's worth of people to look at — does a signal built on *who* a
person deals with find more of the known insiders than the plain 34-feature
scorer we have had since the start?

The first attempt asked: did this person go back to a website nobody else in the
company has ever visited? It found one insider out of seventy. The reason was in
its own pre-written check: in this dataset, *nobody* has a website only they
visit. The data is generated by drawing everyone's ordinary browsing from one
shared pool, so exclusivity simply does not exist in it. The question was fine;
the dataset could not answer it.

So this attempt asked a different question of a different column. We now have a
register of staff email addresses, so: did this person write to a colleague they
had never written to before? That is novelty rather than exclusivity, and it sits
on real addresses rather than planted websites. We wrote down the rule, froze the
code with a fingerprint, said in advance what would count as a pass, and then
looked once.

It found 21 insiders. The old scorer finds 59.

Two things make that a clearer answer than the number alone. First, a coin-flip
comparison: if you just picked 48 people a day at random, you would expect to
stumble on about 35 of them. Ranking by "wrote to someone new" finds 21 — worse
than not ranking at all. Second, all 21 are people the old scorer already finds.
The new signal adds nobody.

The pre-written reading explains it. All 930 innocent people in the dataset have
days where they email someone new. Writing to a new colleague is what working
somewhere looks like. A budget of 48 a day just skims the top of an ordinary
count, and the people at the top are not the insiders.

Both attempts are now spent and neither is rerun. Version 1 still stands at 8 of
12, and S3 is one of the four it does not meet. What is left to say honestly is
this: the register names *who was written to*, and counting those names — new or
exclusive — does not separate these insiders. The things it still cannot name are
which machine and which website a person touched, and that is where any further
identity signal would have to come from.
